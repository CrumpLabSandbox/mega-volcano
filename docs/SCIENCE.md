# How volcanic islands form

Mega-Volcano follows one model island through its whole life (`js/life.js`). It's the classic story of a **hotspot**
island in the middle of an ocean plate, like Hawaiʻi, with the birth borrowed from the best-watched new island,
**Surtsey**. Other ways islands are born (island arcs, mid-ocean ridges) get their own scenes.

## 1. Seamount (stage 0–0.12)

A plume of hot mantle rock rises under the plate and partly melts. Basalt magma works up through the crust and erupts
on the sea floor, 4–5 km down. At that depth the water pressure stops gas from expanding explosively, so lava oozes out
and chills instantly into **pillow lava**: rounded lobes with glassy skins, each one budding the next. Quenched glass
shatters into **hyaloclastite**. The pile grows into a seamount. Hawaiʻi's next island, **Kamaʻehuakanaloa** (formerly
Lōʻihi), is at this stage today, about 975 m below the surface; it may break the surface in tens of thousands of years.

## 2. Breaching the surface (0.12–0.22)

When the vent is a few hundred metres down or less, water flashes to steam where it meets magma. The steam blasts
shred the lava into ash and lapilli: a **Surtseyan** (phreatomagmatic) eruption. Its signature is the black
*cypressoid* or "cock's tail" jet of tephra that arcs out of the sea, beside billowing white steam, and base surges
racing across the water. A cone of loose **tuff** builds above the waves. Waves can erase it in months unless lava
starts to flow.

- **Surtsey**, Iceland: the eruption started on 14 November 1963 on a sea floor 130 m deep and broke the surface the
  next day. It ran until June 1967. Once the vents were sealed from the sea, lava flows armoured the island against the
  waves. It peaked at about 2.7 km² and 174 m high; erosion has roughly halved its area since. Scientists have watched
  plants, birds and insects colonise it, with almost no human visits.
- **Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai**, Tonga: a Surtseyan eruption in 2014–15 built a new tuff cone joining two older islands.
  On 15 January 2022 the volcano blew apart in the largest explosion recorded by modern instruments; its plume reached
  about 57 km, into the mesosphere, and its pressure wave circled the Earth several times.
- **Nishinoshima**, Japan: a new vent emerged in 2013 next to an older island and swallowed it with lava flows.

## 3. Shield building (0.22–0.50)

Once the vent is above water, the eruption turns **effusive**. Hot (about 1,150 °C), runny tholeiitic basalt pours out
from the summit and from **rift zones** on the flanks, in curtains of fire along fissures and as lava fountains. Flows
come in two textures: smooth, ropy **pāhoehoe** and rough, clinkery **ʻaʻā**. Crusted-over flows become **lava tubes**
that carry lava kilometres to the coast. Where lava meets the sea it builds new land (lava deltas and benches), shatters
into black sand, and raises white plumes of **laze** (steam with hydrochloric acid and glass shards).

Flow after flow piles up into a **shield volcano**: broad, gently sloping (a few degrees), like a warrior's shield lying
on the ground. This is where 95% of the island's volume comes from. Mauna Loa rises about 4.2 km above sea level but
about 9 km above the sea floor, and its weight bows the plate down beneath it. Kīlauea's 2018 eruption added about
3.5 km² of new land, filling Kapoho Bay.

## 4. Caldera and cinder cones (0.50–0.62)

As the plate carries the volcano off the hotspot, the supply of magma falls. The summit roof collapses into a
**caldera** over the emptying chamber. The magma left is cooler, gassier and more alkalic, so eruptions are rarer and
spatter out **cinder cones** that dot the upper slopes (Mauna Kea's summit is covered in them). A cap of this post-shield
lava can coat the old shield.

## 5. Erosion and life (0.62–0.80)

The volcano dies. Trade-wind rain carves deep **amphitheatre valleys**; waves cut **sea cliffs** (the Nā Pali coast,
Molokaʻi's 1 km cliffs, among the tallest on Earth). Sometimes a whole flank slides into the sea in a giant landslide.
Seeds drift in on currents and arrive in birds' guts and feathers; ferns, mosses and lichens come first on bare lava,
then forest. In warm seas, **coral** settles on the shallow flanks and builds a **fringing reef** right against the
shore. Rarely, a late burst of **rejuvenated** volcanism (Honolulu's Diamond Head and Punchbowl) adds new cones.

## 6. Sinking (0.80–0.92)

The plate cools and thickens as it ages, so it sinks: the island subsides as it drifts away from the hotspot, about
2–3 mm a year. Erosion lowers it from above. Coral can grow upward faster than that, so the reef keeps pace with sea
level while the island shrinks inside it. The reef becomes a **barrier reef**, with a lagoon between reef and shore
(Bora Bora today).

## 7. Atoll and guyot (0.92–1.0)

The last of the volcano sinks out of sight. Only the reef is left: a ring of coral and sandy islets (*motu*) around a
lagoon, an **atoll**. Charles Darwin worked this sequence out in 1842 from the shapes of reefs alone. Drilling at
Enewetak in 1952 proved him right: it went through about 1.4 km of coral before hitting volcanic basalt. If the island
sinks faster than coral can grow, or drifts into cold water, the reef dies and the flat-topped mountain becomes a
drowned **guyot**.

## The conveyor belt

Because the hotspot stays roughly fixed while the plate moves over it (the Pacific plate moves about 7–10 cm a year
to the northwest), the islands line up in a chain that gets older, lower and more eroded with distance: the Big Island
(under 1 million years), Maui, Molokaʻi, Oʻahu, Kauaʻi (about 5 million years), then atolls like Midway (28 million)
and the drowned Emperor Seamounts. The bend in the chain, about 47 million years old, records a change in the plate's
motion. The scenes in **Hotspot** show this conveyor.

## Other ways to make an island

- **Island arcs**: where one ocean plate dives under another, water driven out of the sinking slab melts the mantle
  above. The magma is sticky, gas-rich andesite, so the volcanoes are steep **stratovolcanoes** with explosive eruptions
  and pyroclastic flows (Japan, the Aleutians, the Lesser Antilles, Anak Krakatau).
- **Mid-ocean ridges**: where plates pull apart, basalt wells up along a rift. Iceland is a ridge sitting on a
  hotspot, so it rises above sea level; fissure eruptions there build long rows of craters.
- **Caldera collapse and rebirth**: a huge eruption can empty a chamber and drop the whole island into the sea, leaving
  a ring of islands around a flooded caldera (Santorini; Krakatau in 1883). A new cone can grow in the middle, as Anak
  Krakatau ("child of Krakatau") did from 1927.
